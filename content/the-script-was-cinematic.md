The script had everything I'd hoped for: camera directions, lighting notes, every cinematic detail. Reading it, I thought: *this could be a great ad.*

Then I generated the video.

It was worse than what I had managed on my own.

I had wanted something original, a cinematic promo for one of our products at Pure Bhutan, where I work. What I got instead was a lesson in the gap between a prompt that sounds impressive and a result you can actually use.

## How a chemistry graduate ended up here

I'm Avinash Biswa. I studied Chemistry (BSc), but most of my work today is graphic design, mainly labels and packaging.

Technology has always pulled at me. In middle school I taught myself some programming, and now I'm coming back to that curiosity as a hobbyist: learning what I can and trying things as I go.

Higgsfield was one of those experiments. I work with visuals every day, so I wanted to see what I could make with it.

## A convincing script, a disappointing result

I'd watched long YouTube videos about pairing Higgsfield with tools like Claude or ChatGPT. The idea was exciting: let an AI assistant write the script, the prompts and the creative direction, then turn all of it into a video.

In practice, the polished instructions didn't reliably produce better footage. Sometimes the result was worse than my own rough attempts.

**A script can describe a brilliant video without producing one.** That was the part I hadn't understood yet.

## What the courses changed

After those first attempts, I took a few of Higgsfield's free courses. They taught me workflows for generating more efficiently and wasting fewer credits.

In some ways the process was simpler than the long tutorials made it look. In another way it was harder: knowing *what* to ask for is not the same as knowing *how* to get a usable result.

So I stopped judging a prompt by how cinematic it sounded, and started paying attention to the workflow behind it.

> **Lesson:** When every experiment costs credits, understanding the workflow matters more than writing the perfect prompt.

## One experiment that worked

While I was exploring, I also tried building an AI agent for Pure Bhutan that could answer questions about our products.

I gave it a knowledge base about our products and paired it with an avatar of a Bhutanese girl. The avatar looked surprisingly realistic, and the agent answered product questions well.

That felt useful in a different way from the video. Here I could see a practical job for AI: helping people understand what we make.

## Still exploring, still spending credits

I'm still learning, and I keep finding new things to try. The disappointing video didn't end my interest. It changed how I approached the next experiment.

The hard part is the cost of trial and error. Credits disappear, including on results you'll never use.

For now, I'm trying to keep the curiosity and get better at the process.

My curiosity is free. My experiments are not.
